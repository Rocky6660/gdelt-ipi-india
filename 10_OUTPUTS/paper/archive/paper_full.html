Abstract

We build an open, reproducible index of news attention to inflation for India from the GDELT Global Knowledge Graph: a balanced core of 104 outlets (54 of them vernacular) drawn from a panel of 714 Indian domains, 2016–2026, measured in the two weeks before each round of the Reserve Bank of India's household inflation expectations survey. Indices built from alternate days correlate at 0.89, a sign of reliability rather than validity. An audit of 1,250 sampled articles, coded twice by a large language model with no human coding, finds that GDELT's inflation tag has precision 0.16 and recall 0.30. Under a rule fixed in advance we switch to a broader price-related theme family. That index correlates 0.54 with Google searches for "inflation" (the original tag: 0.78), but correlations with Hindi search terms are weak, and the index peaks in only one of four pre-specified price episodes, so we treat it as audited but not externally validated and all expectation results as exploratory. In 56 survey rounds, one standard deviation more attention is associated with a 0.25 percentage-point larger change in the median one-year expectation after real-time inflation, the policy rate and survey mode (95% CI 0.07 to 0.43); the wild-bootstrap p-value is 0.058, so the primary estimate does not clear the 5% level. The estimate depends on the control set and the outcome, and the index does not improve a CPI-based forecast. Attention rises after oil-price increases. The contribution is the measurement and its documented limits.

Keywords: inflation expectations; news attention; text data; GDELT; India; household surveys. JEL codes: E31, E58, C55, C81, D84.

1. Introduction

Household inflation expectations are a central input to monetary policy. They enter wage and price setting, they shape the credibility of an inflation target, and in India they have run well above both realised inflation and professional forecasts for most of the inflation-targeting period. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) measures them through its Inflation Expectations Survey of Households (IESH). The survey records what urban households expect but not what they read or hear: the RBI's own analysis of the survey notes that it does not capture households' exposure to media and social interactions (Ruhi, Sharma and Sankaran 2025). News is one plausible channel through which households learn about prices. This paper tries to measure it for India and to document how well the measurement works.

The idea that households learn about inflation from the news is well established. Carroll (2003) models household expectations as updating slowly toward professional forecasts reported in the news. Lamla and Lein (2014) show that the volume and tone of inflation news move German consumers' perceptions and expectations. Larsen, Thorsrud and Zhulanova (2021) find that news topics predict US household expectations and that information rigidity varies with coverage. Aarab, Bańbura, Bobeica and Leguay (2025) build a news-attention-to-inflation indicator for the euro area and show that it tracks consumers' perceived inflation, and Arndt (2024) finds that readers of different newspapers in Germany hold different inflation perceptions. For India, Banerjee, Kanodia and Ray (2021) use text mining to study inflation expectations, and Pratap, Ranjan, Kishore and Bhoi (2021) build news-based sentiment indicators to forecast food inflation. We are not aware of an Indian news measure that covers vernacular outlets, which carry much of India's news readership, or that uses the household survey as its outcome.

GDELT, which machine-codes themes for online news in many languages, offers a way to cover both, at the cost of relying on dictionary-based theme tags whose accuracy for specific economic concepts outside the United States is largely undocumented. That cost is the reason for the audit at the centre of this paper.

The paper makes three contributions, in decreasing order of weight.

  1. Measurement. An open, scripted index of news attention to inflation for India, built from GDELT, covering English and nine Indian languages, with every step reproducible from public data under fixed design rules.
  2. An audit of the measurement. We sample 1,250 articles in eight strata, retrieve their text, and code them under a fixed codebook to estimate the precision and recall of GDELT's inflation tags, by language and by the way the text was retrieved.
  3. Exploratory evidence on expectations. We ask whether attention in the two weeks before a survey round is associated with the change in households' one-year expectation beyond real-time prices, and whether it improves out-of-sample forecasts.

The design, the primary outcome and specification, the validation tests and a rule for switching the index definition were written into an analysis plan dated 17 November 2026, which was fixed (dated and hashed) before any outcome regression was run. We refer to it below as "the analysis plan". The plan was not lodged in a public registry, so we do not describe the study as pre-registered. The audit was completed after the first outcome regressions; Section 9.11 explains how that order bears on interpretation.

Preview of findings. The index is reliable and tracks search interest for the English term, and monthly attention rises after oil-price increases. But GDELT's inflation tag is imprecise and misses most relevant articles; the broader theme family selected by the audit is only slightly better; the index fails the pre-specified episode test; and the association with household expectations is small, sensitive to the control set and the outcome, at the edge of the study's minimum detectable effect, and absent out of sample. We therefore present the index as a measurement with documented limits, not as a validated predictor, and all expectation results as exploratory.

2. Related literature

2.1 Household expectations and information

Households form inflation expectations with limited and costly information. Carroll (2003) shows that US household expectations follow professional forecasts with a lag, consistent with households absorbing news slowly, and that the lag shortens when inflation is in the news. Coibion, Gorodnichenko and Kamdar (2018) review the evidence from survey expectations and document that household expectations are dispersed, biased upward and slow to adjust, which makes the sources of household information a first-order question for policy.

A second strand measures the news directly. Lamla and Lein (2014) use German media content data and find that both the intensity and the tone of inflation reporting move consumers' perceptions and expectations. Larsen, Thorsrud and Zhulanova (2021) extract topics from a large newspaper corpus and show that inflation-related news predicts US household expectations and helps explain information rigidity. Aarab et al. (2025) construct an indicator of attention to inflation in euro-area news and show that it moves with consumers' perceived inflation and remained elevated after inflation fell in 2023. Arndt (2024) links German survey respondents to the newspapers they read and finds that perceptions differ systematically across newspaper types. Our index is closest in spirit to the attention measure of Aarab et al. (2025), but built from an open global database rather than a licensed news archive, for a multilingual press, and audited against article text.

2.2 Text-based inflation measures for India

The Indian literature using text to study inflation is small. Banerjee, Kanodia and Ray (2021) use text mining in an exploratory study of Indian inflation expectations. Pratap, Ranjan, Kishore and Bhoi (2021) construct news-based sentiment indicators and show that they help forecast food inflation. Neither uses the household survey as its outcome, and we are not aware of Indian work that measures vernacular news coverage. Kishor and Pratap (2023) study whether inflation targeting has anchored long-run inflation expectations in India, which provides context for the persistence we find in household expectations.

2.3 The household survey

The IESH has been used to study the level, dispersion and determinants of Indian household expectations. Pattanaik, Muduli and Ray (2019) ask whether household expectations influence wage and price dynamics in India. Ruhi, Sharma and Sankaran (2025) describe trends and determinants of household expectations and the effect of monetary policy on them, and note that the survey does not record media exposure. Our paper complements this work with an external measure of the information environment in which the survey's respondents form expectations.

2.4 GDELT as a research source

GDELT (Leetaru and Schrodt 2013) records events, themes, tone and metadata for a very large share of online news worldwide and is widely used in political science and, increasingly, in economics. Its themes are assigned by dictionary matching, after machine translation for non-English text. Three limitations are well known: coverage changes over time as sources are added or drop out; translation and dictionary matching can mis-tag; and the theme taxonomy mixes concepts from different sources. Accuracy for specific economic concepts in non-Western presses is rarely checked against article text. Our audit adds evidence on that question for one concept and one country, and our design choices (a balanced outlet panel, article shares instead of counts, volume-based validity rules) respond to the first limitation.

3. Institutional background

3.1 Inflation targeting in India

India adopted flexible inflation targeting in 2016. The target is 4% consumer price inflation with a tolerance band of ±2 percentage points, and policy rates are set by a six-member Monetary Policy Committee constituted in 2016. Headline CPI inflation has a large food component, so supply shocks to vegetables, pulses and edible oils move measured inflation sharply, and administered prices for fuel and cooking gas move with government decisions as well as global oil prices.

3.2 The household survey

The IESH asks households in large Indian cities (19 cities, about 6,000 households per round in recent years) for their perception of current inflation and their expectations three months and one year ahead. It has been conducted bimonthly since 2016. Responses are recorded in ranges of one percentage point; the RBI reports medians computed from these grouped responses, interpolated within ranges, and means. The rounds of 2020–21 were conducted by telephone because of the pandemic, and the survey's published documents do not always give exact fieldwork dates before 2020.

3.3 Inflation episodes, 2016–2026

The period covers low inflation in 2017 and 2018–19, an onion-price spike in late 2019, a pandemic period with food-price pressure and very high fuel taxes in 2020–21, a broad rise in 2022 after the invasion of Ukraine, a tomato-price spike in mid-2023, and renewed oil-price pressure in 2025–26. The analysis plan fixed four of these (onion 2019–20, fuel 2021, post-Ukraine 2022, tomato 2023) as validation episodes before the index was inspected around them.

4. Data

4.1 News: the GDELT Global Knowledge Graph

GDELT's Global Knowledge Graph (version 2) records, for each online news article it ingests, the outlet, date, language and a set of themes assigned by dictionary matching. We read two kinds of days.

  • Windows. The 14 days ending the day before each survey round's fieldwork begins, read with the full set of fields. These give the round-level index.
  • Grid. A systematic one-in-four sample of all other days from January 2016 to October 2026, read with fewer fields. These give a monthly series for validation and for the extensions.

The extraction ran on Google BigQuery in capped chunks under a fixed budget of 670 GB and used about 475 GB. An independent recount of 147 days reproduced the extracted counts exactly (no mismatches on 53,335 cells defined by source, day and language). GDELT has no Knowledge Graph data for 16 June to 1 July 2025, so the July 2025 round is excluded.

4.2 Outlet panel

We keep Indian domains, identified from GDELT's domain-to-country file plus a documented manual review of 81 ambiguous domains. Foreign outlets made up 29% of raw volume and are excluded. Of 936 candidate domains, 714 remain, covering about 70% of articles. The index uses a balanced core of 104 outlets present in at least 80% of windows (Table 3; the list is in Appendix B).

4.3 Household expectations

We use the national medians and means of current perceived inflation and of three-month-ahead and one-year-ahead expectations, for rounds from March 2016 to September 2026 (Appendix C lists them). For 26 rounds before 2020 without published exact dates we set the fieldwork start to the first day of the survey month; 37 rounds have exact dates, and we check the date rule with an exact-date-only sample. Six rounds are identified as telephone rounds.

4.4 Prices, policy and markets

Headline and food CPI inflation (base 2012, from the statistics ministry's data service) are taken in real time: the latest release available on the day before fieldwork begins. The policy repo rate comes from 74 RBI policy decisions read from press releases. Brent crude (from FRED) enters the monthly analysis and one robustness row. The analysis plan also listed retail pump prices as a control; no consistent series was available, so it was dropped.

4.5 Google Trends and the GDELT DOC interface

Google Trends gives monthly search interest in India for "inflation", "mehngai" (romanised Hindi for inflation) and its Devanagari spelling, and for twelve item terms used in an extension. Trends rescales every request, and the analysis plan averages three pulls taken on different days. Only one pull was taken, so every Trends number is provisional. GDELT's DOC 2.0 interface gives keyword-based and theme-based article counts for Indian sources from 2017, used as a cross-check.

5. Constructing the index

5.1 Definition

For each outlet and window we compute the share of distinct articles (web addresses deduplicated within a day) carrying an inflation theme among all the outlet's articles, then average these shares with equal weights over the core outlets. Equal weights keep a few high-volume portals from dominating; a volume-weighted version is reported as a robustness check.

5.3 Validity rules

A day is invalid if its global volume or its panel volume falls below a fixed share of its centred 28-day median; 9 of 1,654 sampled days are invalid. A window needs at least 60% valid days; 62 of 63 pass.

5.4 Scale, reliability and variants

The window index is standardised over all rounds in the window file. Under the price family its mean is 2.33% of articles and its standard deviation 0.77 percentage points (maximum 4.92%); in the 56-round estimation sample the standard deviation is 0.69 (Table 1). The baseline index is about half that level (mean 1.14%, standard deviation 0.52 percentage points; minimum 0.36% in May 2021, maximum 2.16% in September 2022).

Indices built separately from odd and even days correlate at 0.89 (Spearman–Brown 0.94). This shows that the index is measured with little sampling noise; it does not show that it measures attention to inflation. Duplicates across days are 0.3% of articles and wire copies 3.6% of inflation articles. 6. Auditing GDELT's inflation tags

6.1 Sample design

We drew 1,250 articles from window days in eight strata (Table 6): tagged and untagged economic articles in English, in Hindi and Marathi, and in other vernaculars; non-economic articles whose web address contains a price word; and other non-economic articles. The years 2015–18 were oversampled by a factor of 1.5 because older links are more often dead. Sampling weights undo the stratification. The non-economic strata were drawn from a 19-day subsample and their weights scaled up to all window days.

6.2 Retrieval

Texts were retrieved live or, failing that, from the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine (a snapshot within 30 days of the article date). Of 1,250 articles, 1,113 (89%) were retrieved, 417 of them from the archive. Retrieval was lower for older articles: by stratum and period it ranges from 0.74 (tagged English articles, 2015–18) to 0.99 (tagged other-vernacular articles, 2019 onward; Appendix Table D1). Articles that could not be retrieved are not imputed; they re-weight the coded articles in their stratum-by-period cell, which assumes that retrieval is unrelated to relevance within a cell.

6.3 Codebook

Each article was coded for four items under a codebook fixed and hashed before coding (Appendix D):

  • relevance: does the article substantially discuss price levels, inflation or the cost of living affecting consumers in India;
  • direction: rising, falling, mixed or none;
  • primary and secondary cause: domestic food supply, domestic fuel and energy, global oil and commodities, exchange rate, monetary policy, taxes and administered prices, wages and demand, corporate pricing, inflation abroad, or other/none;
  • an uncertainty flag.

6.4 Coding protocol

There was no human coding. By design, coding was done twice, in separate passes, by the same large language model (Claude), working through the articles inside an interactive session; the first pass is treated as the truth. The coder saw only the article text and web address, never the stratum, the GDELT tags or the weights. The first pass coded all 1,113 retrieved articles in a fixed order; the second coded the 1,026 articles in the strata that decide the gating metrics, in a seeded random order within blocks. For the first 613 articles of the first pass and 610 of the second, the coder saw excerpts of 800–1,100 characters; to reduce time and cost, later articles were coded from excerpts of 200–300 characters, which may understate relevance for long articles.

Agreement between the two passes was very high: relevance κ = 1.00 on 1,026 articles; direction κ = 0.97 and cause κ = 0.94 on the 118 articles that both passes coded as relevant. Two passes of one model are not independent coders, so this agreement shows consistency of the model with itself, not accuracy.

6.5 Precision and recall

Precision is the share of tagged articles that are relevant; recall is the share of relevant articles that are tagged; F2 weights recall twice as heavily as precision. All are weighted to the sampled population and computed in the "gating frame" (economic articles plus non-economic articles with a price word in the address), with 95% intervals from a stratified bootstrap. The analysis plan required precision of at least 0.5 and recall of at least 0.6.

6.6 The unscreened stratum and the address-only fallback

The non-economic articles without a price word in their address are outside the gating frame; they bound how many relevant articles the frame misses. One of 87 coded articles in that stratum was relevant (exact 95% CI 0.000 to 0.062). Because that stratum is very large, even this low share implies that recall including it could be as low as 0.04, so the frame-based recall figures are upper bounds for the whole news flow.

For the 137 articles with no retrievable text, coding from the web address alone gives precision 0.13 (0.03 to 0.26) and recall 0.56, with F2 0.34; only 7% of addresses were uninformative. Address coding is cruder than text coding and is not used in any decision.

Bounds for non-retrieval. The estimates above assume that, within each stratum-by-period cell, retrieval is unrelated to relevance. Table D2 replaces that assumption with the extremes for the 124 unretrieved articles in the gating frame. If all of them were relevant, baseline precision would rise to 0.264 and recall fall to 0.094; if none were, precision would be 0.146 and recall 0.299. Imputing the address-based codes gives 0.160 and 0.314. Under every assumption precision stays far below 0.5 and recall below 0.6, for both definitions, and the price family keeps the higher F2. The audit result does not depend on how the unretrieved articles are treated.

6.7 What the tags pick up

The audit codes do not classify irrelevant articles by type, so we cannot report counts of false-positive types; we do not characterise them further. Among the 119 articles the first pass coded as relevant, 79 describe rising prices, 26 falling, 6 mixed and 8 no direction. The most common stated causes are domestic fuel and energy (26), taxes and administered prices (24), domestic food supply (19) and global oil and commodities (9); 28 state no cause or another one. Agreement between passes on whether a cause was international was low (κ = 0.33 on 86 articles), so cause coding is not used for claims about international framing.

6.8 The switching rule and its outcome

The analysis plan's rule was: if the baseline tag failed the precision and recall bar, switch to the pre-specified definition with the highest F2; then validate it externally (Section 7); if it failed, try the strict definition; if every candidate failed, keep the audited definition, label the index "audited, not externally validated", and treat all expectation tests as exploratory. The baseline failed, the price family had the highest F2, and both the price family and the strict definition failed external validation. The primary index is therefore the price family, with that label. No threshold, definition or specification was changed after the results were seen.

7. External validation

The analysis plan required the monthly index (i) to correlate at least 0.5 with Google searches for "inflation", and (ii) to peak above the 90th percentile of non-episode months in at least three of the four episodes. It also checked three placebo dates (election results on 23 May 2019 and 4 June 2024, and the Cricket World Cup final on 19 November 2023), where a peak would suggest the index picks up general news volume.

Placebos. In the daily baseline index, the window around the 2024 election results has a mean of 2.31% of articles against a 90th percentile of 2.03% in the surrounding 60 days, so it counts as a peak; the 2019 election and the 2023 cricket final do not. No survey-round window contains 4 June 2024, so this placebo peak does not enter the round-level index.

The extraction kept distinct-address counts only for survey-window days, so this series uses the grid days that fall inside windows: 68 months, 52 to 97 outlets per month, correlation 0.80 with the monthly series. It gives the same result (Table D4): one of four episodes peaks (post-Ukraine), and the raw correlation with searches for "inflation" is 0.42, against 0.59 for the monthly series on the same 68 months. A match on all grid days would require a new extraction.

Conclusion of validation. The episode test fails for every definition tested, so under the analysis plan the index is "audited, not externally validated" and the expectation results below are exploratory. The correlations that pass are correlations between levels of persistent series, which is weak validation, and the Trends evidence rests on one pull.

8. Empirical specification

8.1 The local projection

For survey round t and horizon h ≥ 0 we estimate

Δh yt ≡ yt+h - yt-1 = α + β At + γ1 yt-1 + γ2 Δ yt-1 + δ′ Xt + εt,

where y is the survey's median one-year expectation, At is the window index in the 14 days before round t's fieldwork, standardised over all rounds, and Xt contains real-time headline and food CPI inflation, the change in the repo rate since the previous round, and the telephone-survey dummy (Jordà 2005). β is the association, in percentage points, of one standard deviation more attention. Including the lagged level and change of the expectation absorbs its persistence: unit-root tests cannot reject a unit root in the median expectation (ADF p = 0.12; KPSS does not reject stationarity), while the index is clearly stationary (ADF p < 0.001).

8.2 Timing

Real-time CPI is the latest release available on the day before fieldwork begins, so it is information households could have had. The news window ends on the same day. A robustness row adds next month's CPI, which households could not have known.

8.3 Inference

With 56 overlapping observations, standard errors use the equal-weighted cosine (EWC) estimator with fixed-b critical values (Lazarus, Lewis, Stock and Watson 2018), and p-values come from a wild bootstrap. The intervals reported in the tables are EWC fixed-b intervals; the p-values are wild-bootstrap p-values. In small samples the two can disagree, for example an interval that just excludes zero alongside a p-value above 0.05. The analysis plan uses the bootstrap p-value as the decision statistic.

8.4 The primary cell and secondary results

One cell was designated primary in advance: one-year median, h = 0. Everything else (other horizons, outcomes, definitions and specifications) is secondary. For forecasting we use a recursive pseudo-real-time exercise and the Clark and West (2007) test for nested models.

8.5 Deviations from the analysis plan

Two deviations are disclosed. The planned retail pump-price control was dropped because no consistent series exists; Brent crude enters only as a robustness row. The plan called for three Trends pulls; one was taken.

9. Results

9.1 Primary estimate

Under the price-family index, one standard deviation more attention (0.77 percentage points of articles) is associated with a 0.25 percentage-point larger change in the median one-year expectation (95% CI 0.07 to 0.43; wild-bootstrap p = 0.058; N = 56). The estimate does not clear the 5% level on the decision statistic. Under the baseline tag, which the audit rejected, the same cell is 0.33 (0.02 to 0.64; p = 0.036).

For scale, the change in the median has a standard deviation of 0.72 percentage points in the estimation sample, so the estimate is about a third of a typical round-to-round move. The median is interpolated within 1-pp response ranges, so it moves in small steps, but its changes are coarse; we read the coefficient as an average shift, not a precise continuous response.

Power. With 6 fixed-b degrees of freedom and the primary standard error, the minimum effect detectable with 80% power at the 5% level is 0.25 percentage points per standard deviation, the same size as the estimate. The study is powered only for effects of this size or larger.

Five points stand out.

  • Controls matter. With only the lagged expectation and headline CPI, the coefficient falls to 0.14 under the price family and 0.09 under the baseline. Adding food inflation, the repo rate and the survey-mode dummy raises it. The estimate is conditional on the control set; we read this as specification dependence, not as evidence for either value.
  • The pandemic rounds. Dropping 2020–21 also drops all telephone rounds, so the mode dummy falls out. Under the price family the estimate shrinks to 0.17 and the interval widens; under the baseline the point estimate is almost unchanged (0.30) and only the interval widens.
  • Dating. Rounds with exact fieldwork dates (N = 32) give larger estimates, 0.35 and 0.47, with wide intervals.
  • Construction. Changing the presence threshold to 60% or 90%, or weighting outlets by volume, gives 0.28 to 0.34 with bootstrap p-values from 0.010 to 0.031, so the equal-weight 80% core is not driving the result. Excluding the 2024 election window changes nothing, because no survey window contains it.
  • Placebo outcome. Attention before round t is not associated with the previous change in expectations (0.09, −0.20 to 0.39), as expected if the association is not simply picking up trends in expectations. Adding next month's CPI leaves the coefficient at 0.23, so the association is not just anticipation of the next CPI print.
  • Further checks (Appendix Tables E4 to E7). Adding a linear trend, the change in the rupee–dollar rate, or a post-2022 dummy with its interaction leaves the coefficient between 0.235 and 0.284. The interaction is −0.273 (p = 0.052), so the association before 2022 accounts for the estimate and the slope after 2022 is close to zero; the subsamples before and after 2022 (N = 32 and 24) give 0.255 and 0.105, both imprecise. Windows of the last ten or seven days before fieldwork give 0.293 and 0.260 (p = 0.058 and 0.059). Between 84 and 103 of the 104 core outlets contribute to each window (median 97.5); restricting to windows with at least the median gives 0.192 (p = 0.019, N = 30), and balanced panels of the 37 or 44 outlets present in every window give 0.352 and 0.346 (p = 0.012 and 0.009). None of these changes the reading: the estimate is positive and of similar size, and it clears the 5% level only in subsets or alternative panels that were not specified in advance.

The association is larger when lagged CPI inflation exceeds 6% (14 high-inflation rounds): the interaction is 0.18 (p = 0.016). Leaving out each of the seven high-inflation episodes in turn gives interactions from 0.16 to 0.27, but the intervals of three of the seven include zero (Appendix Table E2). Excluding 2020–21 removes the interaction (0.04), so it rests largely on the pandemic rounds.

9.6 VAR impulse responses

A small Bayesian VAR in Brent, CPI, the index, the median expectation and the repo rate (58 rounds, two lags) gives small responses of the expectation to an index shock (Figure 5). With the index ordered before expectations the response is 0.09 percentage points on impact, falling to about zero by the fourth round and slightly negative after five. With expectations ordered first the impact response is zero by construction and later responses range from −0.06 to 0.02. The ordering drives the impact response, so the VAR adds little beyond the local projections.

9.8 What moves the index

Monthly local projections of the standardised monthly index on price shocks show that attention responds to oil prices but not to CPI surprises. A one per cent rise in Brent is followed by 0.016 to 0.020 standard deviations more attention in the same and next two months (about 0.16 to 0.20 standard deviations for a 10% rise), with intervals excluding zero at several horizons up to nine months. A one-point surprise in CPI inflation, relative to a recursive autoregressive forecast, is followed by responses between −0.04 and 0.14 standard deviations, none distinguishable from zero (Appendix Table E3). News attention in India appears to follow visible fuel-price pressure more than surprises in the headline index. Joint tests that all thirteen responses (h = 0 to 12) are zero do not reject for either shock: the block-bootstrap p-value is 0.926 for CPI surprises and 0.203 for Brent, and the Bonferroni bound on the smallest Brent p-value is 0.121 (Table E7). The individual Brent responses should therefore be read as suggestive.

9.9 Persistence

The median one-year expectation is highly persistent (ADF p = 0.12; KPSS does not reject stationarity), and the index is stationary (ADF statistic −5.95, p < 0.001; KPSS 0.19). The specification in changes with lagged levels is designed for this combination; the placebo-outcome test in Section 9.2 is a check against spurious association from trends.

9.10 Multiple testing

The paper reports 84 estimated coefficients or test statistics from the main analysis (42 local projections, 10 state-dependence rows, 2 forecast comparisons, 26 monthly responses and 4 horse-race rows), plus the robustness and extension rows, and 25 further robustness rows and joint tests in Appendix Tables E4 to E7. They are not independent: they share the outcome, the sample and most regressors. Only one cell was designated primary, and its bootstrap p-value is 0.058 under the definition the audit selected (0.036 under the rejected baseline). We do not apply a multiplicity correction to the secondary results, which should be read as descriptive; given the failed validation, the analysis plan treats all expectation results as exploratory.

9.11 Order of work

The analysis plan was fixed before any outcome regression, but the first outcome regressions used the baseline tag and were run before the audit was complete. The audit then selected the price family under the rule fixed in the plan, and the analysis was re-run with it. Because the switch followed a rule that did not depend on the regression results, and because we report both definitions throughout, the order does not allow the definition to be chosen for its results; but the reader should know that the baseline results were seen first.

11. Discussion

What an attention index can tell a central bank. An attention index measures how much of the news flow is about prices, not what households read or believe. Its value for a central bank is as a timely, cheap indicator of the information environment between survey rounds, and as a way to ask whether expectations move more when prices are in the news. Our results give modest support to the first use (the index tracks English-term search interest and responds to oil prices) and only exploratory, specification-dependent evidence on the second.

Comparison with the euro-area attention indicator. Aarab et al. (2025) report that euro-area news attention to inflation rose sharply in 2022 and stayed elevated after inflation fell. Our Indian index shows the same 2022 rise and persistence. That persistence is also one possible reason our episode test fails: when the general level of attention shifts, fixed percentile thresholds no longer pick out shorter item-specific episodes.

Using GDELT for Indian and vernacular press. GDELT makes multilingual coverage feasible at low cost, and the index built from it is internally reliable. But its inflation tag is imprecise, and recall differs sharply between English and vernacular text. Anyone using GDELT themes for economic concepts in India, or comparing languages, should audit the tags first. A supervised text classifier trained on a human-coded sample would likely improve both precision and recall.

12. Limitations

  1. No human validation. The audit relies on two passes of one language model. Their agreement shows the model's consistency with itself, not accuracy, and later batches were coded from short excerpts. A human-coded subsample of at least 100 articles is the most important missing check.
  2. Imprecise measurement. The inflation tag has precision 0.16 and recall 0.30; the price family is only slightly better. The index measures price-related themes as GDELT tags them, not household reading. Recall differs sharply by language, and the recall figures are upper bounds for the whole news flow. Treating the unretrieved articles as all relevant or all irrelevant does not change this.
  3. Failed external validation. The index peaks in one of four pre-specified episodes for every definition tested. The Trends comparison uses one pull instead of three, and the Hindi-term correlations are weak. Rebuilding the monthly series with the round-level construction gives the same result.
  4. Order of work. The first outcome regressions used the baseline tag and were run before the audit selected the price family. The switch followed a rule fixed in advance, and both definitions are reported.
  5. Small sample and low power. 56 rounds, overlapping fieldwork, medians computed from grouped responses, and a minimum detectable effect equal to the estimate. The primary estimate does not clear the 5% level.
  6. Specification dependence. The estimate roughly halves with a minimal control set and shrinks when the pandemic rounds are dropped. The association is concentrated before 2022; the slope after 2022 is close to zero.
  7. Coverage. Urban households in large cities only; online outlets in GDELT only; one-in-four grid days for the monthly series; the July 2025 round lost to a GDELT outage; six telephone rounds in 2020–21; pre-2020 fieldwork dates set by rule for 26 rounds.
  8. Deviations from the plan. The pump-price control was dropped for lack of data; one Trends pull instead of three; the media-implied salience version was not completed; the English–vernacular split exists only for the baseline tag.
  9. No benchmark comparison. We do not compare the index with existing Indian news-based inflation measures or with an economic-policy-uncertainty-style newspaper count, because neither was available to us in comparable form.
  10. No causal interpretation. Attention may respond to the same price shocks households experience, as the oil-price responses suggest; controlling for real-time CPI does not remove that.

13. Conclusion

GDELT can be turned into an open, reliable, multilingual index of news attention to inflation for India. The index tracks English-term search interest and responds to oil prices. But GDELT's inflation tag captures many articles that are not about consumer prices and misses most that are; the index does not reproduce known price episodes; and its association with household expectations is small, sensitive to the control set, at the edge of what the sample can detect, short of the 5% level, and absent out of sample. The evidence on expectations is exploratory. The measurement and its documented limits are the contribution. A human-coded audit, a supervised classifier in place of theme tags, item-level attention measures for food and fuel, and three Trends pulls are the obvious next steps.

Appendix G. Reproducibility

Pipeline. (1) Plan and log the BigQuery extraction in capped chunks with dry runs; (2) extract window and grid days; (3) reconcile counts with an independent recount; (4) build the outlet panel and the indices; (5) draw the audit sample, retrieve texts, code them, and compute the audit metrics; (6) apply the switching rule and external validation; (7) estimate the expectation models, forecasts and extensions; (8) produce the paper's tables and figures.

Budget and integrity. The BigQuery extraction used about 475 GB of a 670 GB budget. An independent recount of 147 days matched the extracted counts in all 53,335 cells.

Re-run check. A script rebuilds the index from the cached extraction and re-runs the expectation models, the external validation, the switching rule, the extensions and the audit metrics, then compares the outputs with those reported here. Every compared file was byte-identical or numerically identical.
